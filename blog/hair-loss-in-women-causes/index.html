Hair Loss in Women: Common Causes and Next Steps

Short answerWomen lose hair for many reasons: low iron or vitamin levels, thyroid disease, hormonal conditions such as PCOS, childbirth, crash dieting, certain medicines, and inherited female pattern thinning. The cause has to be identified first, because treatment depends on it and some causes are fully reversible.

Shedding and thinning are different problems

Most people lose fifty to a hundred hairs a day without noticing. Trouble starts when the daily count rises for weeks, or when the parting widens and the ponytail feels thinner. The first pattern is usually shedding (telogen effluvium), where many hairs enter the resting phase together after a trigger. The second is more often pattern thinning, where follicles shrink slowly over years.

Shedding tends to show up two to three months after the trigger, which is why women often cannot connect it to the fever, delivery or weight loss that caused it.

Causes doctors look for

  • Iron deficiency and low ferritin, common among Indian women even when haemoglobin looks acceptable
  • Low vitamin D or B12, which is frequent in people who rarely sit in sunlight or follow a vegetarian diet
  • Thyroid disorders, both underactive and overactive
  • PCOS and other hormonal conditions, sometimes with irregular periods or facial hair
  • Childbirth, major illness, high fever or surgery
  • Crash diets and very low protein intake
  • Hair-pulling hairstyles, frequent heat styling and harsh chemical treatments
  • Inherited female pattern hair loss, which usually widens the central parting

Scalp conditions such as heavy dandruff, fungal infection or patchy alopecia areata also cause loss and need their own treatment. In Goa’s humid months, an itchy, flaky scalp is common and sometimes gets blamed for hair fall that has another source.

What an assessment usually involves

A doctor will ask when the loss began, what changed in the months before, your diet, medicines, periods and family history. The scalp is examined, often with a dermoscope, and a gentle pull test may be done. Blood tests commonly include haemoglobin, ferritin, thyroid function and vitamin levels, with hormone tests added when the history suggests it.

Please avoid starting supplements on your own before testing. Taking iron without knowing your level can cause stomach upset and does not help if iron was never the problem.

Treatment depends on the cause

If a deficiency or thyroid problem is found, correcting it is the first step, and shedding often settles over several months once the trigger is removed. Hair growth is slow, so it can take six months or more to judge any treatment fairly. For pattern thinning, options include minoxidil and other medicines prescribed by a doctor, and in selected cases procedures such as PRP. Some medicines are not suitable during pregnancy or breastfeeding, so tell your doctor if either applies.

Hair transplantation is sometimes considered for women with stable pattern thinning and enough healthy donor hair. It is not suitable for every woman, and it is not done when the cause of the loss is unclear or still active.

Habits that make thinning worse

Tight buns and braids pulled every day can cause traction loss along the hairline, and the damage becomes permanent if it goes on for years. Oiling is harmless, but leaving heavy oil on a sweaty scalp overnight in Goa’s humidity can feed dandruff and fungal irritation. Hot irons, frequent colouring and keratin or smoothing treatments weaken the hair shaft and cause breakage, which looks like thinning though the roots are healthy.

Diet matters too. Many families eat little protein at lunch and dinner, and skipping meals to lose weight can trigger shedding a few months later. A plain, regular diet with enough dal, eggs, fish or paneer helps, and your doctor can tell you if any supplement is truly needed.

When to see a doctor

  • Sudden or patchy loss, or bald spots
  • Loss that continues beyond three months
  • Thinning along with tiredness, weight change, irregular periods or skin changes
  • Scalp pain, redness or scaling

Suitability for any treatment is decided at consultation after examination and tests. Results vary from person to person, and no treatment can be promised to work for everyone.
