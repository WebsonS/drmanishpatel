PRP for Hair Loss: What It Is and Who It Suits

Short answerPRP (platelet-rich plasma) is made from a small sample of your own blood, spun to concentrate platelets, then injected into the scalp. Studies suggest it may help some people with early pattern thinning, but responses vary, repeat sessions are usually needed, and it is not a replacement for a transplant or for medicines.

What PRP actually is

Platelets carry growth factors that the body uses in healing. In PRP treatment, a few millilitres of blood are drawn from your arm and spun in a centrifuge. The platelet-rich layer is separated and then injected into the areas of thinning hair. The idea is that these growth factors may support follicles that have shrunk but are still alive.

Who may be considered

PRP is generally discussed for early or moderate pattern thinning, where there are still miniaturised hairs to support. It is less likely to help in areas that have been completely smooth and bald for many years, because follicles that are gone cannot be revived.

  • Early thinning at the crown or along the parting
  • People who want to add to a medical plan with minoxidil or other prescribed medicines
  • Selected patients who are not ready for surgery

It may not be advised if you have a blood disorder, are on blood thinners, have an active scalp infection, or have had certain serious illnesses. Your doctor needs your full medical history before deciding.

How a session goes

The visit usually takes around an hour. Blood is drawn, processed, and then the scalp is cleaned and numbed with a local anaesthetic or cooling. The injections are small and spread across the treated area. Most people go back to routine activities the same day, though some prefer to avoid a wedding or a public event for a day or two in case of redness.

A typical plan is a short series of sessions a few weeks apart, followed by maintenance sessions. The exact schedule depends on your scalp and your response, and it is set by your doctor.

What the evidence says, honestly

Research on PRP for hair is real but mixed. Several small studies report increased hair density in some participants, yet methods differ, preparation techniques are not standardised, and not everybody responds. Anyone who tells you a specific outcome is certain is going beyond what the evidence supports. Hair also needs time: changes are judged over months, not weeks.

Questions to ask before booking

Ask who prepares the PRP, which kit or system is used, how many sessions are planned, and what happens if you see no change. A reasonable clinic will tell you plainly that some people do not respond. Ask also whether the plan includes medicines, because PRP is often used alongside them rather than alone.

Be cautious of anyone who advertises a fixed number of hairs or a promised density. Hair counts depend on the individual scalp, and your doctor can show you progress by comparing standard photographs and scalp measurements taken in the same way at each visit.

Possible side effects

  • Tenderness, mild swelling or headache for a day or two
  • Small bruises at injection points
  • Rarely, infection or a reaction to the local anaesthetic
  • No visible benefit in some people

PRP does not stop the underlying cause of hair loss. A proper diagnosis comes first, since low iron, thyroid disease or a scalp condition can make any treatment less useful. Whether PRP, medicines, a transplant or simple observation is right for you is decided at consultation, and a written estimate is provided after assessment.
