Otoplasty: Ear Correction for Children and Adults

Short answerOtoplasty reshapes prominent or unusually formed ears by setting them closer to the head. It is commonly done from about five to seven years of age, once ear growth is nearly complete, and it can also be done in adults. It is usually a day-case procedure with a few weeks of protection afterwards.

Why ears stand out

Prominent ears usually come from a missing or flat fold in the outer ear (the antihelix) or from deep cartilage in the bowl of the ear, and sometimes both. They often run in families and are present from birth. Other ear differences, such as a cupped ear or a torn earlobe from heavy earrings, are also treated by ear surgeons.

Ear moulding in newborns

In the first weeks of life, ear cartilage is soft because of the mother’s hormones. Some ear shapes can be corrected with a splint or moulding device if started very early, within the first few weeks. After that window the cartilage firms up and moulding no longer works. If you notice a difference in a newborn, ask your paediatrician early.

When to consider surgery for a child

Ears reach around nine-tenths of adult size by about five to six years, so surgery is generally done from that age onward. Many parents choose the time before school starts or in the early school years, when teasing begins. The wish for the operation must be the child’s as much as the parents’; a child who is not bothered, or is afraid, can be given time. There is no medical urgency.

What the operation involves

A cut is placed behind the ear, where the scar is hidden in the fold. The cartilage is reshaped with stitches or small adjustments to create a natural fold, and the ear is pinned closer to the head. For children the operation is generally under general anaesthesia; for adults local anaesthesia with sedation is often an option. Each ear can take about an hour.

Healing

  • A bulky head dressing for the first days, followed by a soft headband worn at night for several weeks
  • Bruising and swelling that fade within a couple of weeks
  • Contact sports and swimming avoided for about a month or as advised
  • Sleeping on the back or with the head raised at first
  • Schools in Goa will usually allow a short absence; plan around exams and monsoon holidays

Talking with your child

Explain the operation in simple words, describe the dressing and the headband, and let your child ask questions. Children who understand what will happen cope better. Avoid promising that teasing will end, as that is not in anyone’s control. Be ready to postpone if your child is anxious or unwell.

For adults the reasons are often long-standing, and the operation is usually done with local anaesthesia. Adults can heal a little more slowly, and cartilage is firmer, but the principles are the same.

Risks and honest limits

Possible problems include bleeding or a collection of blood under the skin, infection, an ear that moves back toward its previous position, mismatch between the two ears, a thickened scar, and changes in feeling. Ears are rarely perfectly symmetrical, even in people who have not had surgery. A second operation is needed in some people.

Suitability and timing are decided at consultation. Dr. Patel examines the ears, speaks with the child and parents together, and gives a written estimate afterwards.
